# Natural academic language and sentence rhythm

## One main job per sentence

Assign each sentence one primary function:

- establish context;
- state a claim;
- report evidence or a result;
- interpret evidence;
- compare or contrast;
- state a limitation;
- describe a method step;
- express an implication;
- connect two adjacent reasoning steps.

A sentence may contain a qualifier, condition, or contrast when it directly defines the same proposition. Split it when it independently reports multiple results, mixes observation with interpretation, changes topic, or chains several causal steps.

Do not reduce “one job” to uniformly short prose. A longer sentence is useful when a main proposition needs tightly coupled conditions or qualifications. The reader should still be able to identify its subject, main verb, and single controlling point immediately.

## Prefer scholarly synthesis over narration

Write around the scientific relationship, result, or inference rather than the author's sequence of actions. Compress procedural narration when the procedure itself is not the point.

- In the Abstract, lead with the problem, approach, principal finding, and implication; omit discovery chronology.
- In the Introduction, synthesize prior work by unresolved question or conceptual tension rather than listing studies one by one.
- In Results, state the finding or comparison before supporting analysis when this preserves accuracy. Avoid repeated `We first... We then... Finally, we...` narration unless experimental order affects interpretation.
- In the Discussion, organize around what the results mean, how they alter current understanding, and where their boundary lies.
- In Methods, favor direct reproducible description. Do not sacrifice procedural clarity merely to sound more abstract.

Aim for high information density, not abstraction for its own sake. Use precise nouns and verbs, remove redundant setup, combine repeated background into one synthesis, and retain the concrete evidence that makes a condensed claim intelligible. Do not replace narrative clutter with excessive nominalization or opaque noun stacks.

## Build deliberate sentence rhythm

Use length to match function:

- Use a short sentence for a principal finding, decisive contrast, paragraph pivot, or carefully bounded emphasis.
- Use a medium sentence for most explanations, comparisons, and evidence statements.
- Use a longer sentence selectively when several qualifiers belong to one relationship and separating them would distort meaning.

At paragraph scale, vary sentence openings and lengths only when the content calls for it. Never enforce a short-long-short sequence, a sentence-length quota, or a fixed alternation rule. Let rhetorical function, information dependency, emphasis, and the relevant exemplar section determine sentence length. Avoid long runs of sentences with nearly identical syntax. Read the paragraph aloud or inspect punctuation and sentence lengths; revise abrupt or monotonous sequences.

Do not create rhythm by adding filler. Scientific content, not ornamental phrasing, should determine sentence length.

## Use connectors by logical function

First identify the relation, then decide whether a connector is needed. Lexical continuity, parallel syntax, or a direct subject can often carry the relation more naturally than an adverb.

| Relation | Possible realization | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | `however`, `whereas`, `by contrast`, direct juxtaposition | Using contrast when the second sentence merely adds detail |
| Concession | `although`, `despite`, `nevertheless` | Treating a limitation as a contradiction |
| Consequence | `therefore`, `thus`, `as a result`, explicit causal verb | Claiming causality from sequence or correlation |
| Addition | direct continuation, `also`, `in addition` | Repeatedly opening paragraphs with `Moreover` or `Furthermore` |
| Specification | `specifically`, colon, explicit noun phrase | Using `notably` without explaining what is scientifically notable |
| Evidence-to-interpretation | `these results suggest`, direct interpretive subject | Repeating `This indicates that` after every result |
| Limitation | `however`, `although`, explicit boundary statement | Appending a generic limitations sentence unrelated to scope |
| Implication | `this finding implies`, explicit consequence | Inflating a local result into a broad promise |

Avoid using the same sentence-initial connector repeatedly within or across adjacent paragraphs. Do not vary connectors through thesaurus substitution alone; vary sentence structure and make the logical subject explicit.

## Detect AI-sounding academic prose

Revise a phrase when it is generic, evaluative without evidence, detachable from the manuscript, or repeated as a template. Common warning signs include:

- `delve into`, `in the realm of`, `in today's rapidly evolving ...`;
- `pivotal`, `crucial`, `groundbreaking`, `transformative`, `remarkable`, or `unprecedented` without a defined comparison;
- `intricate interplay`, `multifaceted nature`, `holistic understanding`, or `dynamic landscape` used as vague abstraction;
- `underscores the importance of`, `highlights the significance of`, or `sheds light on` when a concrete result can be stated;
- `paves the way for`, `holds great promise`, `opens new avenues`, or `represents a paradigm shift` without specific next steps or demonstrated impact;
- `it is important/worth noting that` when the following proposition can stand directly;
- automatic paragraph openings with `Moreover`, `Furthermore`, `Additionally`, or `Notably`;
- repeated frames such as `This finding suggests that ...`, `These results demonstrate that ...`, and `Taken together ...` regardless of evidential function.

Do not enforce a blind blacklist. Words such as `robust`, `significant`, `novel`, `complex`, or `critical` can be correct when they have a defined technical or statistical meaning. Require the sentence to show the relevant criterion, comparison, or evidence.

## Replace templates with content

Use these transformations as reasoning patterns, not canned replacements:

- Replace importance claims with the specific consequence for measurement, mechanism, performance, theory, or practice.
- Replace novelty adjectives with a factual statement of what differs from prior work.
- Replace vague demonstratives (`this`, `these findings`) with the named result when ambiguity is possible.
- Replace generic transitions with a repeated key term, an explicit relationship, or no connector when sequence is already clear.
- Replace stacked nouns and nominalizations with an actor and precise verb.

## Naturalness audit

For every revised paragraph, check:

1. Does each sentence have one identifiable main job?
2. Are observation, interpretation, and implication separated when they make distinct claims?
3. Do sentence lengths and openings vary for a reason rather than a pattern?
4. Does every connector name a valid relationship, and could the prose be clearer without it?
5. Are any generic evaluative phrases detachable from the paper's evidence?
6. Does the paragraph sound like a domain expert explaining evidence rather than a template describing “significance”?
7. Does the paragraph synthesize the scientific point rather than recount the author's actions, except where method or sequence matters?
